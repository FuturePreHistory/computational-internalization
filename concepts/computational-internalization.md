# Computational Internalization

计算性内化

## Definition

Computational internalization refers to a condition in which a machine-maintained computational relation has acquired relatively stable pre-reflective experiential efficacy for a subject, while important computational and translational conditions supporting that efficacy remain externally maintained, versioned, and modifiable.

The central structure can be represented as:

`R → I(R) → T → E`

followed by continuing external modification of:

`R`, `I(R)`, and/or `T`

after `E` has already become part of the subject's relatively stable experiential organization.

The result is a distinctive asymmetry:

the subject may possess a stable intuition without simultaneously possessing the relation-identity conditions, generation history, version history, or modification authority that sustain that intuition.

## What is internalized

Computational internalization does not necessarily mean that a complete computational model has been transferred into the brain or independently reproduced by the subject.

What becomes internalized is experiential efficacy.

A relation may become part of how similarity, salience, expectation, bodily preparation, or possibilities for action are organized, even while the computational structure responsible for maintaining and updating that relation remains outside the subject.

Computational internalization is therefore compatible with continued dependence on external infrastructure.

## Two temporal processes

Computational internalization introduces two coupled but potentially asynchronous histories.

Technical version time:

`(Rₜ, Iₜ, Tₜ) → (Rₜ₊₁, Iₜ₊₁, Tₜ₊₁)`

Subject adaptation time:

`Eτ → learning, habituation, and practice → Eτ₊₁`

The indices `t` and `τ` need not advance together.

A computational system may be updated first, while the subject gradually absorbs the consequences through subsequent use.

Conversely, experiential habits may persist after the technical system has already been removed, replaced, or substantially revised.

Computational internalization therefore creates the possibility that technical history and experiential history diverge.

## Externalization of relation-identity conditions

In ordinary conceptual practice, a subject or community may be able to preserve the identity of a relation through public definitions, rules, measurements, examples, and independent reconstruction.

In strong computational-internalization cases, some of these identity conditions may remain partly external.

Consider:

`R₁ → I(R₁) → T₁ → E₁`

followed by:

`R₂ → I(R₂) → T₂ → E₂`.

From the subject's perspective, `E₁` and `E₂` may feel like the continuous development of the same intuition.

At the computational level, however, the relation supporting that intuition may have changed substantially.

The subject's experiential continuity therefore cannot by itself establish computational relation identity.

## Experience–relation correspondence drift

When experiential continuity is preserved while the computational relation underlying that experience crosses a relevant identity boundary, the resulting condition can be described as experience–relation correspondence drift.

The subject may continue to experience what appears to be the same form of similarity, risk, anomaly, proximity, or practical intuition even though the computational structure sustaining it has changed.

This phenomenon should be distinguished from ordinary model updating.

A model can change while preserving the relation properties relevant to the experience.

Correspondence drift becomes significant when changes in `R`, `I(R)`, or `T` alter the relation identity relevant to the experiential function while experiential continuity conceals that alteration.

## Referential drift

A further problem arises when a stable public concept `K` continues to name an experience or practical distinction after the underlying computational relation has changed.

A simplified structure is:

`Λ₁ → E₁ → K`

followed by:

`Λ₂ → E₂ → K`.

If the same public term continues to be used despite a substantive change from `Λ₁` to `Λ₂`, the system may exhibit relation-reference drift.

Conceptual continuity then no longer guarantees continuity of the computational relation or even complete comparability of the corresponding experiential structures.

## Shared computational history

Computational internalization may carry histories that exceed the learning history of any current subject.

A deployed computational relation can incorporate:

- previous users' behavior;
- institutional classifications;
- past success and failure labels;
- optimization objectives;
- earlier technical translations;
- historical deployment conditions;
- feedback already shaped by previous versions of the same system.

This accumulated background can be called computational history.

A new subject may therefore acquire an experiential distinction whose generating conditions already contain compressed traces of collective, institutional, and technical history.

## Standardization and divergence

Shared computational infrastructure can produce two apparently opposite effects.

It can standardize experience by exposing different subjects to similar relations and translation structures.

It can also individualize experience when `R`, `I(R)`, or especially `T` are adapted to personal histories, behavioral data, or continuously updated profiles.

The two effects can coexist.

Subjects may share a common computational infrastructure while receiving different experiential weighting of similarity, salience, expectation, and action possibilities.

This creates a new problem for public comparison: identical linguistic concepts need not guarantee identical underlying experiential organization.

## Contestability

Computational internalization changes where contestability must occur.

When a judgment remains explicit, disagreement can often be directed at a proposition, category, measurement, or rule.

When a computational relation has acquired pre-reflective experiential efficacy, part of the relevant dispute may have to move toward the infrastructure that generated the experience.

Contestability may therefore require access to:

- model versions;
- target relation definitions;
- translation mappings;
- calibration procedures;
- modification histories;
- exposure histories;
- alternative models;
- alternative translations;
- independent validation channels.

This does not eliminate conceptual or public disagreement.

It adds an infrastructural layer to it.

## Conceptualization openness

Computational internalization need not permanently trap a relation below the conceptual level.

A possible trajectory is:

`R → I(R) → T → E → stable practical distinction → K → theory`.

Here `K` represents a later public concept capable of naming, comparing, criticizing, and transmitting at least part of the relation.

A second trajectory is also possible:

`R → I(R) → T → E`

while no sufficiently effective public `K` emerges.

The subject can then act on a stable intuition that remains difficult to express, independently reconstruct, or publicly dispute.

A responsible system should therefore preserve conceptualization openness: the continuing possibility that experiential differences can later be named, compared, externalized, and brought into public argument.

## Technical and experiential reversibility

Technical reversibility does not imply experiential reversibility.

A model or translation mechanism may be disabled, replaced, or rolled back while learned experiential structures remain active.

For long-duration or high-impact deployments, exit therefore involves more than terminating technical access.

It may also require:

- longitudinal experiential baselines;
- comparison with alternative translation systems;
- retraining;
- restoration of alternative judgment pathways;
- identification of residual effects from previous versions.

Computational internalization consequently creates a form of version history that can persist in experience after the technical system itself has changed.

## Epistemic limitation

Computational internalization does not make a computational relation epistemically valid.

A subject can internalize a relation that is:

- biased;
- unstable;
- spurious;
- institutionally distorted;
- generated from endogenous feedback;
- optimized toward an inappropriate objective.

The existence of a stable intuition therefore cannot serve as independent evidence for the validity of the computational structure that produced it.

In recursive systems, validation must occur at a scale large enough to include the major feedback loops that generate and update the relation.

## Relation to category migration

Category migration concerns the acquisition of experiential function.

Computational internalization concerns the persistence of that experiential function under continuing external computational maintenance and modification.

The distinction can be summarized as:

`Category migration:`  
Where does the relation function?

`Computational internalization:`  
Once it functions inside experience, where are the conditions that preserve, identify, update, and modify it located?

The first is primarily a question of functional position.

The second is a question of dependency, temporality, relation identity, and control.

## Canonical formulation

Computational internalization occurs when a computational relation acquires stable pre-reflective experiential efficacy while the relation-identity conditions, technical translation, generation history, or modification authority necessary to sustain that efficacy remain partly external to the subject and continue to evolve through an independently versioned computational infrastructure.
